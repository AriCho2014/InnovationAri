What type of foliage is most difficult for archaeologists to excavate and how do they get past those foliages?
By Aarize, Aarush, Ian, Arjun, Purav, and Arinjay

For the New Vistas Center For Education Engineering Club: Innovation

Invasive plants

Invasive species can be particularly problematic due to their aggressive and pervasive root structures.

● Kudzu (Pueraria montana): Known as "the vine that ate the South," it spreads rapidly and obscures features.

● Horsetail (Equisetum arvense): Its deep rhizomes intrude and disrupt layers.

● Garlic Mustard (Alliaria petiolata): Alters soil composition and dominates forest floors.

Mature Trees and Root Systems

Deep roots from large trees disrupt archaeological layers, mix soil, and can destroy fragile artifacts. Roots change soil chemistry, affect drainage, and can even displace entire sections when uprooted.

Challenges of Excavating Roots

Roots create tight webs that make digging difficult, cause "floralturbation" (disruption of soil layers), and risk damaging fragile artifacts. Features may also be obscured by root systems.

Modern Solutions

Archaeologists now use LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) to see beneath dense foliage.

● LiDAR scans from planes or drones create detailed 3D maps that can reveal hidden features under trees.

● This has led to discoveries of ancient cities in jungles without needing to cut vegetation.

Non-Invasive Methods

● Geophysical surveys such as ground-penetrating radar and magnetometry reveal subsurface structures.

● Surface surveys and shovel testing help locate artifacts under grassy areas.
