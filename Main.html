What type of foliage is most difficult for archaeologists to excavate and how do they get past those foliages?

Invasive plants


Invasive species can be particularly problematic due to their aggressive and pervasive root structures.

● Kudzu (Pueraria montana): Famously known as "the vine that ate the South," kudzu can spread up to a foot a day. Its dense network of roots and runners can completely obscure archaeological features and make an area impenetrable to excavation.

● Horsetail (Equisetum arvense): In Arctic environments, this plant's deep-penetrating rhizomes can intrude and disrupt archaeological layers.

● Garlic Mustard (Alliaria petiolata): This invasive weed can dominate forest floors with its extensive root system, outcompeting native plants and altering the soil composition.

Mature trees with deep, strong root systems can be a serious obstacle for archaeologists, as the roots can grow directly through archaeological deposits.


● Physical disruption: As tree roots grow and expand, they can physically push aside and move artifacts and features, destroying the archaeological context. When a tree dies and its roots decay, voids are left in the soil, which can cause further soil and artifact movement.

● Windthrow damage: When a tree is uprooted by wind, its entire root plate and the surrounding soil are lifted. This can bring buried artifacts to the surface or cause significant, localized damage to near-surface archaeology.

● Chemical changes: Root systems can change soil chemistry and drainage patterns, which affects the preservation of buried organic materials.

Thick, deep, and extensive root systems from large trees are the most difficult foliage for archaeologists to excavate. The deep, entangled roots obstruct the ground, mix soil layers, and can damage buried features and artifacts, complicating excavation and interpretation.

Challenges of excavating roots:


● Physical barrier: Extensive root networks form a tight web beneath the surface, making it difficult for archaeologists to use trowels and other tools to dig precisely.

● Disruption of stratigraphy: As roots grow, they can displace soil, artifacts, and features, a process called "floralturbation". This disrupts the chronological layers of a site (the stratigraphy), leading to misinterpretation of when and how artifacts were deposited.

● Risk of damage: The fine, delicate work of archaeology is challenged by the tough, woody nature of roots. Removing them increases the risk of accidentally damaging fragile buried features, such as postholes or human remains.

● Obscured features: Roots can grow through or around archaeological features, such as building foundations or pit houses, making them difficult to see and interpret.
